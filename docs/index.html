The line is read left to right. Every character is accepted until the trailing comma, where the reader refuses: unexpected '}', expecting a JSON value.

01

I keep my place.

A position is three numbers: an offset into the text, a line, and a column. Every step carries them forward. A tab moves to the next stop — with the default width of eight, a tab at the start of a line puts the next character in column 9 — and a character above the basic plane counts once, the way a reader counts it, not the way a machine stores it.

The position is not a side effect of reading; it is an answer. position is a parser like any other, so a grammar can return where it got to.

02

I tell you what I wanted.

When a choice fails, I do not report the last branch I tried. Every branch that failed at the same place contributes its expectation, and the message names them together: one position, one sentence, all the alternatives.

Naming a parser with label does not hide the alternatives — it replaces the character classes of one branch with the reader's word for it, which is what a person reading the message needs.

1:1: unexpected 'c'; expecting 'a' or 'b'

1:1: unexpected 'c'; expecting 'a' or 'b'

a refusal, in full — position, what was found, what would have done

03

I commit when I have read.

Reading is a commitment. Once I have consumed a character, a later or will not undo it: the text has been read, and the failure is about what comes next. That is what keeps a grammar from trying every alternative at every offset.

attempt is how a grammar says the opposite — give the character back, and let the other branch have it.

1:2: unexpected 'c'; expecting "ab"
Ok("ac")

Every parser stops somewhere. The only question is what it can tell you when it does.

04

I take the table you give me.

Precedence does not have to be a tower of functions, one per level. It can be a list of lists — tightest binding first — from which the expression parser is built bottom up: postfix, prefix, right associative, left associative.

Associativity is in the constructor, not in the order of the list, so ^ folds to the right and - folds to the left without a second grammar.

2^3^2 = 512

05

I wait for the rest.

Text rarely arrives in one piece. A feeder takes it in chunks and answers Needs while the end of what it has is still in the middle of something. Feed the rest and the same grammar finishes the job — no second parser, and nothing that was settled is read twice.

The stage at the top of this page is that loop: one document, held short at every character, and the two answers a reader can give about a prefix.

Needs(1) → Needs(1) → Ok("hello")
